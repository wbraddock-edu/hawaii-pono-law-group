Estate Planning Guide for Hawaii Families

Estate Planning · Apr 24, 2025 · 6 min read

Estate planning is something most people intend to do but postpone until a crisis makes them regret not having done it. The truth is that everyone with assets, a family, or both needs a basic estate plan — and Hawaii has some specific provisions that make local planning particularly important.

Why Hawaii Families Need an Estate Plan

Without a will or trust, your assets will be distributed according to Hawaii's intestate succession laws — which may not reflect your actual wishes. More importantly, if you have minor children and no will designating a guardian, the court will appoint one. Courts try to make good decisions, but a judge who doesn't know your family is not the same as your own clear instructions.

The Core Documents

A basic estate plan for a Hawaii family includes: a Last Will and Testament (directs how your assets are distributed and who will care for minor children), a Revocable Living Trust (can help your estate avoid probate, which is a public and sometimes slow process in Hawaii), a Durable Power of Attorney (designates someone to manage your financial affairs if you're incapacitated), and an Advance Health Care Directive / Healthcare Power of Attorney (designates someone to make medical decisions and specifies your wishes about end-of-life care).

Hawaii Probate

Probate — the court process of administering a deceased person's estate — can be time-consuming and expensive in Hawaii. Assets held in a living trust pass directly to beneficiaries without going through probate. For smaller estates ($100,000 or less in personal property), Hawaii offers a simplified probate procedure. Real property typically goes through probate unless held in a trust or with a right of survivorship. Many Hawaii families with property choose to hold real estate in a living trust specifically to avoid the probate process.

Hawaii-Specific Considerations

Hawaii has unique land tenure issues — the distinction between fee simple and leasehold property is crucial for estate planning purposes. Hawaii's Native Hawaiian homelands (DHHL lots) have specific succession rules. Multigenerational households are common in Hawaii's culture, and estate plans should account for this reality. International families with assets in both Hawaii and other countries need cross-border estate planning.
