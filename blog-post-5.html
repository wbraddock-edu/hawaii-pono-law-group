Hawaii Divorce Law Overview

Family Law · Apr 7, 2025 · 6 min read

Going through a divorce is one of the most difficult experiences a person can face. Understanding how Hawaii's divorce laws work can help you navigate the process more effectively and make informed decisions that protect your interests and those of your children. This is an overview — for advice specific to your situation, please consult with a Hawaii family law attorney.

Grounds for Divorce in Hawaii

Hawaii is a no-fault divorce state, meaning that neither party needs to prove wrongdoing by the other to obtain a divorce. The grounds for divorce in Hawaii include: irreconcilable differences (the most common ground, essentially meaning the marriage has broken down with no reasonable prospect of reconciliation) and certain specific circumstances like living apart for two years. Hawaii courts do not consider marital misconduct (such as infidelity) in determining property division or alimony, though it may be relevant to some custody decisions.

Property Division

Hawaii is an equitable distribution state, meaning marital property is divided fairly but not necessarily equally. Marital property generally includes assets and debts accumulated during the marriage. Separate property — assets owned before marriage or received as gifts or inheritance during the marriage — is typically not subject to division. However, Hawaii courts examine each case individually, and the lines between marital and separate property can become blurred over a long marriage.

Child Custody in Hawaii

Hawaii courts make custody decisions based on the best interests of the child. Hawaii law distinguishes between legal custody (the right to make major decisions about the child's welfare, education, and medical care) and physical custody (where the child primarily lives). Hawaii courts generally favor joint legal custody and encourage parents to cooperate in parenting. A parent's desire to relocate out of Hawaii with children can create significant legal complications in divorce cases.

Alimony (Spousal Support)

Hawaii courts may award alimony based on factors including the length of the marriage, each spouse's financial resources and earning capacity, the standard of living established during the marriage, and the need for support. Permanent alimony is rarely awarded except in long marriages or where one spouse is unable to become self-supporting. Rehabilitative alimony, to help a spouse gain education or training to become self-sufficient, is more common.
